Approach

The category on the label might say “instructional design,” but the actual job is narrower and more useful than that: I make complex things usable.

The through-line

Across everything I build — eLearning courses, internal project plans, performance-support tools, leadership pitches — the same thing is happening underneath. I take material that’s dense, flat, or overwhelming and make it navigable. I find the exact points where people get stuck, and I design the friction out of those moments.

That’s a broader remit than “build a course,” and it’s the reason I don’t brand myself narrowly. But it isn’t vague, either: every project stays inside one boundary — designing experiences and systems that make complex things usable. If it doesn’t do that, it isn’t the work.

How I actually work

I start by locating the stuck points — the places where a learner, a stakeholder, or a reader loses the thread. Those points, not the content outline, drive the design. From there I choose the lightest format that removes the friction: sometimes that’s a scenario, sometimes a job aid, sometimes a one-page plan that finally makes a decision obvious.

I’m comfortable in authoring tools and comfortable in code, which means I’m not boxed in by what a template will let me do. And I build with accessibility from the start — semantic structure, keyboard navigation, real alt text, sufficient contrast — because “usable” has to mean usable for everyone, not most people.

What it’s good for

If you’ve got material that people keep bouncing off — onboarding that doesn’t stick, a process no one follows, training that covers everything and teaches nothing, a strategy that’s clear in your head but not on the page — that gap is exactly where I work.
